3x Rent Rule Explained

Short answer

The 3x rent rule asks for gross monthly income of at least three times the monthly rent: required income = rent × 3. For example, $1,500 rent needs $4,500 gross monthly income. There is no universal federal 3x law, and 40x is a separate New York City listing rule, not a statute — check the official source.

Last verified: 2026-10-25. This is general information, not legal advice. City ordinances may be stricter and may override state rules. Check the official source for your city and state before acting.

What 3x means

Under a 3x requirement, a landlord or listing asks that your gross monthly income — income before tax and deductions — be at least three times the monthly rent. The formula is simple: required income = rent × 3. For $1,500 rent, that is $4,500 per month, or $54,000 per year. The idea behind the multiple is a rough affordability screen: if rent takes more than a third of gross income, the landlord worries the tenancy is less sustainable. It is a screening practice, not a universal law, and this guide does not assert a federal 3x rule.

The 2.5 times rent test is looser than 3x: it lets a lower income qualify for the same rent. The 40x rent rule in New York City looks stricter only because it is annual: $1,500 × 40 = $60,000 per year, which is $5,000 per month — higher than the 3x monthly figure of $4,500. The 40x figure is a New York City listing rule, not a statute, and it is not presented here as a law. That is why the three must never be conflated: 40x is an annual-income test often described in New York City listings, while 2.5x and 3x are monthly-income tests. Can a landlord ask for 3x?

People often search for the three-times-the-rent law and ask, "Do you have to make 3 times the rent to qualify?" There is no universal federal 3x law asserted in this guide: whether a landlord can require it depends on the landlord, the lease, and local rules. Short answer: it depends. Some landlords and listings use an income multiple as one screening criterion; others do not, and local rules may affect what screening criteria can be applied, how they must be disclosed, or whether exceptions apply. This site does not assert a universal rule that a landlord may or may not require 3x in every state or city, because no such universal rule is verified here. If a listing requires 3x, ask what income counts (gross or net, whose income in a shared household, whether benefits or other sources count), get the requirement in writing, and check the official source for your city and state before assuming it applies or does not apply to you.

What if you do not meet 3x?

Not meeting a multiple does not automatically end the application. Some landlords accept a higher deposit where the law allows, a guarantor, proof of savings, or a longer rental history — but whether any of those is available or lawful depends on the lease and local rules, so do not assume. The honest arithmetic step is to check what rent does fit your income: the Rent Affordability Calculator shows the 30% rule, 2.5x, and 3x maxima side by side. A rent that fails 3x may still pass 2.5x, and the 30% rule may give a still lower figure. Compare all three, then check the lease and the official source.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a monthly income with a 40x annual figure — the units must match before any comparison is meaningful.
  • Using net (take-home) income against a gross-income multiple, which makes the test look stricter than the landlord stated it.
  • Assuming that passing 3x guarantees approval — landlords may apply other criteria, and local rules may still matter.
  • Assuming that failing 3x is unlawful, or that requiring 3x is unlawful, without checking the official source for that city and state.

Formula in both directions

Most people use the formula forward: required income = rent × 3. The reverse is just as useful when you are searching: maximum rent at 3x = gross monthly income ÷ 3. Both directions use the same numbers, and checking both can catch a slip. For example, $6,000 gross monthly income divided by 3 gives a maximum rent of $2,000 at 3x, and $2,000 multiplied by 3 gives $6,000, which confirms the pair. If you work in annual figures, convert first: $72,000 gross annual income divided by 12 is $6,000 per month, and $6,000 divided by 3 is $2,000 rent. Never compare an annual income directly with a monthly multiple, or a monthly income directly with the annual 40x figure, without converting one of them first.

On $6,000 income, 3x allows $2,000 rent, but 30% of $6,000 is $1,800. That $200 gap matters when you are budgeting, because a rent that passes a landlord's 3x screen can still leave less room for other costs than a 30% budget would. More worked examples

Example A: two earners with gross monthly incomes of $3,200 and $2,800, if the listing adds household income, give $6,000 combined. At 3x that supports $2,000 rent, because $2,000 × 3 = $6,000. If the listing counts only one earner, the same household supports only $1,066 rent on $3,200 income at 3x, because $3,200 ÷ 3 = $1,066.67, rounded down for searching. That single difference — combined or single income — changes the search more than any other detail in this guide, which is why it must be asked, not assumed.

Example B: a renter earns $4,800 gross per month and is looking at $1,700 rent. Required income at 3x is $1,700 × 3 = $5,100, so $4,800 falls $300 short. At 2.5x, required income is $1,700 × 2.5 = $4,250, so $4,800 passes by $550. Example C: a renter earns $5,400 gross per month. Maximum rent at 3x is $5,400 ÷ 3 = $1,800, maximum at 2.5x is $5,400 ÷ 2.5 = $2,160, and maximum under a 30% rule is $5,400 × 0.30 = $1,620. Example D, annual check: $3,000 rent under the separate 40x New York City listing rule needs $120,000 annual income, because $3,000 × 40 = $120,000, which is $10,000 per month — well above the $9,000 monthly figure that 3x requires for the same rent. Keep the annual and monthly tests in separate columns whenever you compare them.

Edge cases: bonuses, overtime, and a rent rise

Bonuses and overtime raise the same question as variable income: does the listing count them, average them, or exclude them? A year with a large one-time bonus can make an annual average look higher than a typical month, while a month with heavy overtime can make a single pay stub look higher than a sustainable average. Ask how the listing treats each source, and do not build a tenancy budget on a bonus that may not repeat. A rent rise raises a related question: a household that met 3x at the old rent may not meet it at the new rent, because the required income rises with the rent. On $1,800 rent, required income at 3x is $5,400; if rent rises to $2,000, required income rises to $6,000, a $600 increase. That arithmetic does not by itself decide whether a rise is lawful or whether a renewal will be offered — those depend on the lease and the official source — but it shows why a renewal budget should be re-run at the new rent, not carried over from the old one.

Where income varies — shift work, seasonal work, or commission — a single good month and a single bad month give different answers, so the listing’s method for averaging or verifying income matters more than any one pay stub. Ask the listing, in writing, what period it averages over and what documents it accepts, and keep the answer with your application; this guide asserts no universal averaging rule. The same caution applies to household income: whether two earners’ incomes are added together, and whose income counts for a shared home, depends on the listing and local rules, so ask that too, in writing, before you apply.

What 3x does not tell you

Clearing 3x is not an approval: landlords may apply other criteria and local rules may still matter. Failing 3x does not prove a requirement is unlawful, and requiring 3x does not prove it is lawful — both questions depend on the listing, the lease, and the official source for that city and state. The multiple also does not measure your full budget: it ignores debts, childcare, transport, and other fixed costs, so a rent that passes 3x can still be tight in practice. Treat 3x as screening arithmetic, not as a personal affordability verdict, and run a full monthly budget alongside it. For the wider set of rent rules, the pillar guide is Rent Rules Explained.

Frequently asked questions

What does 3x the rent mean?

Gross monthly income of at least three times the monthly rent: required income = rent × 3.

Is 40x the same as 3x?

No. 40x is a separate annual-income test often described in New York City listings: required annual income = rent × 40.

Can landlords ask for 3 times the rent?

It varies by landlord, lease, and local rules. Check the official source for your city and state; this guide asserts no universal law on the point.

What is the maximum rent I can afford at 3x on $5,400 a month?

$1,800, because $5,400 ÷ 3 = $1,800. At 2.5x the maximum is $2,160, and under a 30% rule it is $1,620 — compare all three before you search.

Should I use gross or net income for 3x?

Use gross monthly income, before tax and deductions, unless the listing states a different definition in writing. Using net income makes the test look stricter than the listing stated it.

Do two earners' incomes get added together?

It depends on the listing and local rules. Ask explicitly whether household income is combined and whose income counts, and get the answer in writing before you apply.

How does 3x compare with the 30% rule?

At exactly 3x, rent is 33.3% of gross monthly income, so 3x allows a higher rent than the 30% rule for the same income. On $6,000 income, 3x allows $2,000 rent while 30% allows $1,800.

Is 40x just a stricter version of 3x?

No. Because 40x is an annual test and 3x is a monthly test, the two must not be compared directly without converting units. It is a listing rule, not a statute.

What if my income varies month to month?

Ask the listing in writing what period it averages over and what documents it accepts. This guide asserts no universal averaging rule, so the listing's stated method, checked against the official source, is what you need.
