30-Day vs Actual-Days Proration: Which Method Is Fairer?

Short answer

The 30-day method always divides rent by 30; the actual-days method divides by the real number of days in that month (28, 29, 30, or 31). For the same 16 days at $1,800 rent, actual days gives $929.03 in a 31-day month while the 30-day method gives $960.00. Your lease decides which method applies.

Last verified: 2026-10-25. This is general information, not legal advice. City ordinances may be stricter and may override state rules. Check the official source for your city and state before acting.

Proration methods at a glance

MethodFormulaExample: $1,800, 16 days
Actual daysRent ÷ days in the month × days occupied$929.03 in a 31-day month
30-dayRent ÷ 30 × days occupied$960.00
365-dayRent × 12 ÷ 365 × days occupied$946.85

Two tenants can pay different amounts for the same number of days in the same home, simply because their leases use different proration methods. The 30-day method divides rent by 30 every month. The actual-days method divides by the real number of days in that month — 28, 29, 30, or 31. Understanding the difference helps you check a bill, negotiate a lease, and avoid a dispute that is really just a method mismatch.

How the daily rate differs

Take $1,800 monthly rent. On the 30-day method, the daily rate is always $60.00. On actual days, it is $64.29 in a 28-day February ($1,800 ÷ 28), $62.07 in a 29-day leap February, $60.00 in a 30-day month, and $58.06 in a 31-day month. In other words, the 30-day method and the actual-days method agree exactly only in a 30-day month. In a 31-day month, the 30-day method charges more per day than actual days; in February, it charges less per day. Neither is inherently wrong — but only one will match your lease.

Month-by-month comparison

For 16 days occupied at $1,800 rent: in a 31-day month, actual days gives $929.03 and the 30-day method gives $960.00, a $30.97 difference in the landlord’s favor under the 30-day method. In a 30-day month, both give $960.00. In a 28-day February, actual days gives $1,028.57 for 16 days, while the 30-day method gives $960.00 — here the tenant pays less under the 30-day method. In a 29-day leap February, actual days gives $993.10 for 16 days, versus $960.00 on the 30-day method. The direction of the difference flips with the month, which is why a tenant who moves in February and a tenant who moves in October can reasonably reach opposite conclusions about which method is “better.”

Which method should a lease use?

For tenants, the actual-days method is the easiest to verify against a calendar and feels most intuitive: you pay for the days in that month at that month’s daily rate. For landlords with many units, the 30-day method is simpler to administer because the daily rate never changes. For tenancies that run for a full year with a mid-year rent change, the 365-day method can be cleaner. There is no universally required method on this site’s verified rules, so the practical advice is the same for both sides: name the method in the lease, show the day count and the working on the move-in statement, and agree in advance how the first and last days are counted.

The 365-day method is a third option: it smooths month-length differences but matches no single month’s calendar exactly, so a tenant expecting a calendar-based figure may query it — what matters is that the lease states the method clearly.

Checking a bill that uses the other method

If your bill does not match your calculation, first check whether the method differs — that explains most mismatches. Then check the day count, the month’s length, and whether rounding was done at the end or at each step. If the method in the bill is not the method in your lease, raise that in writing with both calculations side by side. If the lease is silent, ask for the method to be confirmed in writing before you pay, and keep the reply with the lease.

A full set of month lengths at $2,100 for 10 days

The table below holds rent and days occupied fixed and changes only the month, so the method effect is isolated. At $2,100 for 10 days, the 30-day method always gives $700.00 and the 365-day method always gives $690.41. Actual days moves with the calendar: $677.42 in a 31-day month, $700.00 in a 30-day month, $750.00 in a 28-day February, and $724.14 in a 29-day leap February. The spread between the cheapest and most expensive actual-days result here is $72.58, even though rent and days never change. That spread is the whole reason a move-in month matters: a tenant who compares a February bill with an October bill without adjusting for month length will think one of them is wrong, when both may follow the same actual-days rule.

Short stays and longer partial months at $1,500

Short stays show the same pattern in miniature. For 7 days at $1,500 rent, actual days in a 31-day month gives $338.71, the 30-day method gives $350.00, and the 365-day method gives $345.21 — a range of only $11.29. For 20 days at the same rent, the figures are $967.74, $1,000.00 and $986.30, a range of $32.26. The gap grows with days occupied because the daily-rate difference is multiplied by more days. That is worth remembering when a landlord or tenant argues about a one-week stay as if the stakes were the same as a three-week stay: the method matters more, in dollars, the longer the partial month runs. Rounding also deserves a note here. Each figure above rounds only the final total to cents; rounding the daily rate first — for example, writing $48.39 instead of $48.3871 for $1,500 ÷ 31 — then multiplying can shift the total by a few cents, which is enough to make two otherwise identical calculations disagree on paper.

February at $2,400: the biggest swing in the year

February produces the largest method gap for a given rent, because its denominator is the smallest. For 12 days at $2,400 rent in a 28-day February, actual days gives $1,028.57, while the 30-day method gives $960.00 and the 365-day method gives $946.85. The actual-days tenant pays $68.57 more than the 30-day tenant for the same 12 days. In a leap February the gap narrows but does not vanish: $2,400 ÷ 29 × 12 = $993.10 on actual days, still $33.10 above the 30-day figure. A landlord who quotes a February move-in price from a 30-day spreadsheet and a tenant who checks it with an actual-days calculator will therefore disagree by tens of dollars without either of them making an arithmetic error. The fix is not better arithmetic; it is naming the method before the money changes hands.

Edge case: a stay that straddles two months

Some partial periods cross a month boundary, and each month must then be prorated separately at its own length. Take $1,800 rent for a stay from 28 January to 14 February. January contributes 4 days (28, 29, 30, 31 January) at $1,800 ÷ 31 = $58.06 per day, giving $232.26. February contributes 14 days at $1,800 ÷ 28 = $64.29 per day, giving $900.00. The combined total is $1,132.26. A single 30-day calculation for the same 18 days gives $1,800 ÷ 30 × 18 = $1,080.00, which is $52.26 lower — but it is only comparable if the lease uses the 30-day method for both months. Never apply one month's denominator to days that fall in another month, and never average the two daily rates: January days are January days, February days are February days, and the bill should show both lines separately so either side can check them.

Edge case: occupying all 31 days under a 30-day method

A literal 30-day calculation for 31 days occupied gives more than a full month's rent: $1,800 ÷ 30 × 31 = $1,860.00. In practice, many leases cap a partial month at the full monthly rent, or define the first month differently, but this guide does not assert a universal cap — it flags the question to ask before signing. If your lease uses the 30-day method and you will occupy a whole 31-day month, ask in writing whether the amount is capped at $1,800 or charged as $1,860, and keep the answer with the lease. The same check applies in reverse for a full 28-day February: a literal 30-day calculation for 28 days gives $1,680.00 for $1,800 rent, which is less than a full month, so again the lease wording, not the formula alone, decides the bill.

What the comparison does not tell you

A method comparison is arithmetic, not a ruling on what you owe. It does not tell you which method your lease requires, whether a city ordinance prescribes a method, whether a deposit or fee is also due, or whether a rent cap limits a later increase. It also does not settle fairness in any absolute sense: actual days tracks the calendar, the 30-day method tracks administrative simplicity, and the 365-day method tracks an annual average. Each can be the fairer choice in a different tenancy, depending on what was agreed. If a bill uses a method your lease does not name, the next step is a written question citing the lease clause, not a claim that one method is generally fairer than another. For any state or city rule beyond the verified rules in Rent Rules Explained, check the official source.

Frequently asked questions

Which method is more common?

Actual-days is widely used, but 30-day is also common. Your lease decides.

Do the methods ever agree?

Yes — in a 30-day month, actual-days and 30-day give the same result for the same rent and days.

Is the 365-day method fair?

It gives a consistent yearly daily rate. Whether it is fair in your case depends on what your lease says and what you agreed.

What happens if I move in on the 31st?

You occupy 1 day in that month. At $1,800 rent, actual days gives $58.06, the 30-day method gives $60.00, and the 365-day method gives $59.18. The method gap is under $2 for a single day, which is why single-day disputes are rarely about the method and usually about whether that day counts at all.

Can I switch methods mid-tenancy?

Only by agreement, confirmed in writing. If a lease names one method, a landlord or tenant cannot unilaterally switch to a cheaper method for a later partial month. Any change should state the new method, the date it takes effect, and how the month in which it changes is split.

Why does February flip which method is cheaper?

Because February has the fewest days, its actual-days daily rate is the highest. The 30-day daily rate stays fixed, so in February the 30-day method is cheaper for the tenant, while in a 31-day month it is more expensive. The winner flips with the calendar, not with the tenant.

Does the 30-day method ever charge for more than a full month?

On a literal calculation, yes: 31 days at $1,800 rent gives $1,860.00. Whether your lease caps the charge at the full monthly rent is a lease question, so ask it in writing before you occupy a full 31-day month under a 30-day clause.

How do I check a bill in under a minute?

Find the rent, the days occupied, and the month length. Divide rent by the method's denominator, multiply by days, and compare with the bill to the cent. If it differs, check the day count first, then the method, then rounding — in that order, because day-count errors are the most common.
