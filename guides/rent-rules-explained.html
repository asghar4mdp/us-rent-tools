Renting a home involves more than the monthly rent figure on a listing. When you move in mid-month, renew a lease, pay a deposit, or pay a few days late, a different rule may apply — and that rule often depends on your lease, your city, and your state. This guide explains the four topics tenants and landlords ask about most: prorated rent, rent caps, security deposits, and late fees. It gives you the formulas, worked examples, and the questions to check before you pay or sign anything.

Prorated rent: paying for part of a month

Prorated rent is rent for only part of a month, most often when you move in or move out mid-month. Instead of paying a full month, you pay for the days you actually occupy the home. There are three common methods, and the choice matters. The actual-days method divides the monthly rent by the number of days in that calendar month and multiplies by the days occupied. The 30-day method always divides by 30, even in a 28- or 31-day month. The 365-day method converts annual rent to a daily rate and multiplies by the days occupied.

For example, with $1,800 rent and a move-in on 16 October in a 31-day month, the actual-days method gives $1,800 ÷ 31 × 16 = $929.03, because 16 days are occupied from 16 October through 31 October inclusive. The same move-in under a 30-day method would give $1,800 ÷ 30 × 16 = $960.00. That $30.97 difference is why you should never assume the method: ask which one your lease or landlord uses, get it in writing, and check that the day count matches the move-in and move-out dates you agreed.

Common mistakes include counting the move-in day twice, forgetting that February has fewer days, and prorating a month that the lease says is not prorated. Some leases charge a full month regardless of the move-in date, or prorate only the first month and not the last. Read both clauses. If you are a landlord, state the method, the start date, and the day count on the lease or move-in statement so the tenant can check the arithmetic.

Rent caps: how much can rent go up?

A rent cap limits how much rent can increase over a set period, usually 12 months. Only some states and cities have one, and the formulas differ. California’s statewide cap under AB 1482 (Civil Code 1947.12) is the lower of 5% plus local CPI and 10% per 12 months, for covered properties, and local ordinances may be stricter. Washington’s cap under HB 1217 (RCW 59.18.700) is the lower of 7% plus CPI and 10%, with no increase in the first 12 months. For example, $2,000 rent with 3% CPI in California gives a maximum of $2,000 × 1.08 = $2,160, because 5% + 3% = 8% is below the 10% ceiling.

A cap is not the same as rent control, and it does not mean rent cannot rise at all. Exemptions, notice periods, and local rules can change the result. A property covered by the state cap may also be covered by a stricter city ordinance, which can override the state figure. If your state or city is not listed as verified on this site, do not assume there is no cap and do not assume a cap from another state applies — check the official source for your city and state. Security deposits: how much, and when do you get it back?

A security deposit is money held to cover unpaid rent or damage beyond normal wear and tear. The amount a landlord can charge, what it can be used for, and how quickly it must be returned all vary by state. In California, AB 12 (Civil Code 1950.5) generally limits the deposit to one month’s rent, with a two-month exception for small landlords as defined in the statute, and requires return within 21 calendar days. In Texas, there is no statutory cap on the amount, but the deposit must generally be returned within 30 days, with the forwarding-address trigger in Property Code 92.103 and 92.107 playing a central role — the tenant’s forwarding address starts the clock in the way the statute describes.

In every state, the practical steps are similar: photograph the home at move-in and move-out, keep a copy of the move-in checklist, give a forwarding address in writing, and keep receipts for cleaning or repairs you paid for. A landlord who withholds part of a deposit should normally provide an itemised statement. If you disagree with a deduction, ask for the itemisation first, then check the deadline and procedure in the official source for your state before taking further steps. Do not deduct a deposit from your last month’s rent unless your lease or state law clearly allows it.

Late fees and grace periods

A late fee is a charge for paying rent after the due date. Whether a fee can be charged at all, how long the grace period is, and how the fee is calculated depend on the lease and on state law. Oregon, for example, under ORS 90.260, provides a 4-day grace period, requires the fee to be in a written agreement, and uses a structure of 5% per succeeding 5-day period as one statutory structure — it should not be presented as a single flat 5% overall cap. Other states may have different grace periods, caps, or no statewide rule at all, in which case the lease and local ordinance matter most.

If rent is late, pay as soon as you can, keep proof of payment, and communicate in writing. If you are a landlord, do not add a fee that is not in the written agreement, and do not charge a fee during a grace period the law provides. Repeated late fees can add up quickly, so a tenant who expects a delay should ask early about a payment plan rather than waiting for fees to accrue. A fee calculator can show the arithmetic, but it cannot decide whether a particular fee is lawful in your situation.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming a rule from one state applies in another — rent caps, deposit caps, and late-fee rules are state- and city-specific.
  • Using the wrong proration method or day count, especially in February or a 31-day month.
  • Forgetting a forwarding address, which can delay or complicate a deposit return in states such as Texas.
  • Paying a late fee without checking whether it was in the written agreement or whether a grace period applied.
  • Treating a calculator result as a legal ruling — calculators do the arithmetic; the law and your lease decide whether it applies.

What to check before you act

Before you pay, sign, or dispute anything, work through this short checklist: read the relevant lease clause in full; identify your city and state and check the official source for both; confirm the dates, day counts, and amounts in writing; and keep copies of everything you send and receive. If the amount is large, the facts are disputed, or a deadline is about to pass, consider getting advice from a local housing organisation or a qualified professional. This guide and its calculators are a starting point for understanding the numbers, not a substitute for that advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is prorated rent?

Prorated rent is rent for part of a month, calculated from the days you occupy the home. The method — actual-days, 30-day, or 365-day — changes the result, so confirm which method applies before paying.

Is there a nationwide rent cap?

No. Rent caps exist only in some states and cities, with different formulas and exemptions. California and Washington have statewide caps described in this guide; for other places, check the official source.

How long does a landlord have to return a deposit?

It depends on the state. California generally allows 21 calendar days; Texas generally allows 30 days after the forwarding-address trigger. Other states differ, so check the official source for your state.

Can a landlord charge a late fee immediately?

Not always. Some states provide a grace period, and some require the fee to be in a written agreement. Oregon, for example, has a 4-day grace period under ORS 90.260.
