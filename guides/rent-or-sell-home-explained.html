Rent or Sell Your Home?

Short answer

Should you sell your home now or rent it out? There is no universal answer — it depends on your numbers. In the worked example in this guide, selling now leaves $126,000.00 after selling costs and the mortgage, while renting it out for 5 years and then selling leaves $201,966.88 in total, so renting ends $75,966.88 ahead — but only on that example's assumptions. Run your own numbers on the Rent or Sell Calculator, test the assumptions that could flip the answer, and talk to a tax professional before you decide.

This is general information, not legal advice and not financial advice. Check the official source for your city and state, and talk to a tax professional about your own situation, before acting.

The two sides of the decision

When you move out of a home you own, you usually face the same fork in the road: sell it now and take the money, or keep it, rent it out, and sell it later — or not at all. Both sides are reasonable, and both can go wrong for reasons that have nothing to do with how nice the house is. The mistake is to compare them on feeling alone: selling feels final and tidy, renting feels like keeping an asset that pays you every month. Neither feeling is a calculation, and the calculation is what this guide is for.

Selling now has one great strength, and it is worth naming plainly: certainty. You agree a price, you pay the selling costs, you repay the mortgage, and the money left over is yours to use — for the next home, for paying down debt, or for anything else. There is no tenant to find, no midnight repair call, no month when the rent does not arrive. There is also one great cost: once you sell, you no longer own the home, so if its value rises over the next few years, that rise belongs to someone else. You also pay the selling costs now, in full, rather than spreading them over a later sale.

Renting it out has the opposite shape. You keep the home, so you keep any rise in its value, and every mortgage payment slowly reduces what you owe, which builds equity even in a month when the rent only just covers the costs. But you also keep every cost and every risk of ownership — the mortgage, the taxes, the insurance, the repairs — and you add the work of being a landlord on top. The rent may cover all of that, or it may not. In the worked example in this guide, it does not cover it at first: the monthly cash flow in year 1 is -$152.40, which means the owner pays $152.40 a month out of pocket on top of everything else. That does not automatically make renting the wrong choice, because cash flow is only one of three forces, but it is exactly the kind of fact that feelings hide and arithmetic shows.

That is why the only honest way to compare the two sides is to put them on the same footing: what do you walk away with if you sell now, and what do you walk away with if you rent it out for a set number of years and then sell? The worked example in this guide does exactly that, over a 5-year horizon, and every figure in it comes from the same set of labelled example values, so you can follow each step and then change any value to your own. The example home is worth $400,000, with a $250,000 mortgage, a $1,500 monthly mortgage payment at a 6% annual rate, rent of $2,400 a month, taxes of $4,800 a year, insurance of $1,800 a year, maintenance of $2,400 a year, no HOA fee, 5% vacancy, 8% management, a 5-year horizon, 3% annual appreciation, and 3% annual rent growth. Selling costs in the example are 6%. None of those figures is presented as typical — they are the example's inputs, and your own inputs will be different. The point of the example is to show how the comparison works, not to tell you what your home is worth or what your rent should be.

Keep one more distinction in mind as you read. Selling now gives you a single lump sum. Renting gives you a stream of monthly cash flow, some of it negative, plus a lump sum later when you eventually sell. To compare them fairly, this guide adds the two parts of the rent path together: the total net rental cash flow over the 5 years, plus what you walk away with when you sell after 5 years. Only then can the two totals be placed side by side. Comparing a monthly rent figure with a sale price, without doing that addition, is the most common error in this decision, and it makes renting look better than it is — because it counts the rent and forgets the costs, or counts the rent and forgets that the later sale still has selling costs and a mortgage to repay.

What selling costs

Selling a home is not free, and the selling costs are the first thing to subtract before either side of the decision can be judged. In the worked example, selling costs are set at 6% of the home value as an example figure. On a $400,000 home, that is $24,000, which leaves $376,000 before the mortgage is repaid. The mortgage balance in the example is $250,000, so selling now leaves $376,000 − $250,000 = $126,000.00. That $126,000.00 is the number the rent path has to beat. It is also worth noticing how large the selling-cost figure is on its own: $24,000 is ten months of rent at $2,400 a month, and it is paid whether the home sells quickly or slowly.

What makes up selling costs varies from sale to sale, and this guide does not fix a percentage for anyone else's home. In general, selling costs can include agent commission, closing costs, repairs or preparation work done before listing, and concessions agreed with the buyer, such as a contribution toward the buyer's closing costs. Some of those are negotiated, some depend on the condition of the home, and some depend on local practice, so two identical homes in different places can cost different amounts to sell. The honest move is to get a realistic selling-cost figure for your own home — from an agent's estimate, from a recent comparable sale, or from the calculator's input — and to use that figure in the comparison, rather than borrowing the example's 6%.

There is a second point about selling costs that people often miss: you pay them on the later sale too. Renting the home out for 5 years does not avoid selling costs; it only postpones them, and postpones them onto a different home value. In the worked example, the home is worth $463,709.63 after 5 years, and selling costs at the same example rate of 6% come to $27,822.58 on that later value — more in dollars than the $24,000 paid on a sale today, because the value being sold is larger. The later sale still leaves more money overall in this example, because the home's value has grown by more than the extra selling cost, but the selling cost is not a one-time penalty on selling now. It applies to whichever sale you eventually make, and any comparison that subtracts it from one side only is rigged.

Finally, remember that the sell-now figure is a lump sum you can use immediately, while the rent path's money arrives over 5 years and at the end. This guide does not put a value on having the money sooner — it simply compares the two totals — but the timing matters to real decisions. If you need the money from the sale to buy your next home, selling now may be the only practical option regardless of what the 5-year comparison says. If you do not need it, the comparison in this guide is the right place to start.

What renting it out costs

Renting a home out costs more than the mortgage, and the full list is what decides whether the rent covers the outgoings. This section works through each cost in the worked example, one at a time, using the year-1 figures, so you can see where every dollar goes. The rent in the example is $2,400 a month, but the owner does not collect all of it, and the costs do not stop at the mortgage payment.

The mortgage payment is the largest single cost: $1,500 a month in the example, at a 6% annual mortgage rate. It is also the only cost on this list that builds your wealth at the same time as you pay it, because part of each payment reduces the mortgage balance. That split matters later, in the three-forces section, but for cash flow purposes the full $1,500 leaves your account every month, whatever part of it is interest and whatever part is principal. A lower mortgage payment would change the cash flow directly; a higher one would do the same in the other direction. If your own mortgage payment is different, that alone can change the sign of the monthly cash flow.

Property taxes and insurance come next, and both are costs of owning the home whether it is rented out or lived in. In the example, taxes are $4,800 a year, which is $400 a month, and insurance is $1,800 a year, which is $150 a month. Both can change over time — tax assessments and insurance premiums both move — but in this worked example they are held flat across the 5 years, so the year-1 monthly figures of $400 and $150 are the same figures used in year 5. If your own taxes or insurance are higher, or if they rise during the horizon, the cash flow will be worse than the example's. Check your own tax bill and your own insurance quote, including whether your insurer charges differently for a rented home than for a home you live in, before you trust any cash-flow figure.

Maintenance is the cost people most often underestimate, because it arrives in lumps rather than in neat monthly amounts: a water heater one year, a roof repair the next, a broken appliance in between. In the example, maintenance is set at $2,400 a year, which is $200 a month, as a labelled example figure — not as a claim about what maintenance costs in general. Spread evenly, it looks small. In real life it is not spread evenly, and a single bad year can wipe out several months of rent. Budgeting a monthly figure for it, as the example does, is a way of making sure the lumpy reality is counted at all, rather than ignored until the bill arrives. There is no HOA fee in the example, so that line is $0 — but if your home has one, it belongs in the same list, every month, without exception.

Then come the two costs that only exist because the home is rented out: vacancy and management. Vacancy, at 5% in the example, means the home is assumed to be empty and earning nothing for 5% of the time — roughly three weeks a year. That does not mean the rent is $2,400 and then a separate $120 vacancy bill arrives; it means the rent actually collected averages $2,400 × 95% = $2,280 a month. Management, at 8% in the example, is charged on the rent collected, so it is 8% of $2,280, which is $182.40 a month. A property manager handles finding tenants, collecting rent, and dealing with repairs, and that service costs money whether you hire a manager or pay for it with your own time. If you manage the home yourself, the 8% does not vanish — it becomes your evenings and weekends instead, and this guide counts it as a cost either way, because your time is not free.

Put the year-1 month together and the picture is clear. Rent collected is $2,280.00. The costs are: mortgage $1,500, taxes $400, insurance $150, maintenance $200, HOA $0, and management $182.40 — a total of $2,432.40 going out. The monthly cash flow is $2,280.00 − $2,432.40 = -$152.40. The rent does not cover the costs in year 1, and the owner pays $152.40 a month out of pocket to keep the home rented. That is not a disaster on its own, and it is not the end of the comparison, but it is a fact that has to be faced before the decision is made: in this example, renting the home out costs money every month at the start, and anyone who expected the rent to be pure profit would be wrong.

If you are comparing your own home with this example, start with your own mortgage payment — it is the number most likely to change the answer.

The three forces that decide it

Here is the heart of the guide. The rent path is not decided by the monthly cash flow alone. It is decided by three forces working together, and in the worked example two of them pull in renting's favour while one pulls against it. Once you can name the three forces and measure each one, the final gap of $75,966.88 stops being a mystery and becomes simple addition.

The first force is appreciation: the home's value rising over time. In the example, appreciation is 3% a year, so after 5 years the $400,000 home is worth $400,000 × 1.03 × 1.03 × 1.03 × 1.03 × 1.03 = $463,709.63. That is a rise of $63,709.63, but the owner does not keep all of it, because selling costs are charged on the higher later value. After the example's 6% selling costs, the appreciation the owner actually keeps is $63,709.63 × 94% = $59,887.05. That is the largest of the three forces in this example, and it is also the least certain, because nobody can guarantee what a home will be worth in 5 years. Appreciation is a hope with a number attached to it, not a promise, and the next section shows what happens when it is zero.

The second force is principal paydown: the mortgage balance falling with every payment. In the example, the mortgage starts at $250,000 and, after 5 years of $1,500 monthly payments at a 6% annual rate, the balance is $232,557.49. That means $250,000 − $232,557.49 = $17,442.51 of principal has been paid off, and that $17,442.51 is equity the owner now holds instead of owing. Notice that this happens even though the monthly cash flow is negative at first: the tenant's rent and the owner's top-up together make the mortgage payment, and part of every payment buys back a piece of the home. Principal paydown is the quietest of the three forces — it never shows up in a monthly cash-flow figure — and it is the one most often forgotten. It is also the most reliable of the three in one sense: as long as the mortgage is paid, the balance falls, whatever the housing market does.

The third force is the net rental cash flow itself: all the rent collected over the 5 years, minus all the costs of renting the home out. In the example, the monthly cash flow starts at -$152.40 in year 1, but the rent grows at 3% a year while the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and maintenance are held flat, so the monthly figure improves every year: -$89.47 in year 2, -$24.66 in year 3, $42.10 in year 4, and $110.87 in year 5. Add up all 60 months and the total net rental cash flow over 5 years is -$1,362.68. The rent path costs the owner $1,362.68 out of pocket across the whole 5 years — a small drag, but a drag all the same. That is the force pulling against renting in this example, and it is small only because the later years turn positive and nearly cancel out the early losses. If the rent had not grown, the drag would have been much larger, as the next section shows.

Now put the three forces together, and the final gap appears. Appreciation after selling costs contributes $59,887.05. Principal paid contributes $17,442.51. Net rental cash flow contributes -$1,362.68. Add them: $59,887.05 + $17,442.51 − $1,362.68 = $75,966.88. That is exactly the gap between the two totals: selling now leaves $126,000.00, the rent path leaves $201,966.88 in total, and $201,966.88 − $126,000.00 = $75,966.88. The decomposition is not a second calculation — it is the same answer, split into its three causes, so you can see which one is doing the work. In this example, appreciation does most of it, principal paydown does a solid share, and cash flow takes a little bit back.

That table is also the right way to think about your own decision. If someone tells you renting is better because "the rent covers the mortgage," ask about the other two forces: what happens if the home does not appreciate, and how much principal is actually being paid down? If someone tells you selling is better because "the rent doesn't cover the costs," ask the same question in reverse: how much appreciation and principal paydown are being given up by selling now? A decision made from one force alone is a guess. A decision made from all three, with your own numbers, is a comparison. Try it with your own figures on the Rent or Sell Calculator and look at the same three-way split.

One final check on the rent path's total, so nothing is hidden. Selling after 5 years leaves $463,709.63 × 94% − $232,557.49 = $203,329.56. Add the total net rental cash flow of -$1,362.68, and the rent-path total is $203,329.56 − $1,362.68 = $201,966.88. Compare that with selling now at $126,000.00, and renting ends $75,966.88 ahead — on this example's assumptions, and only on them. Change the assumptions, and the answer changes, which is the subject of the next section.

The assumptions that flip the answer

Every figure in the worked example depends on assumptions, and four of them matter more than all the rest: appreciation, rent growth, the horizon, and selling costs. This section changes each one in turn, holding everything else at the example's values, so you can see how far the answer moves — and which assumption it is most sensitive to. These are what-if illustrations built from the same example numbers, not predictions about any real home.

Start with appreciation, because it is the biggest force and the most uncertain. If appreciation is 0% instead of the example's 3% a year, the home is still worth $400,000 after 5 years. Selling then leaves $400,000 × 94% − $232,557.49 = $143,442.51, and the rent-path total is $143,442.51 − $1,362.68 = $142,079.83. That is still $16,079.83 ahead of selling now at $126,000.00 — because the principal paydown of $17,442.51 is larger than the cash-flow drag of $1,362.68, so renting still wins even with no appreciation at all. That is a useful surprise: in this example, renting does not need the home to rise in value to come out ahead. But the margin is much thinner, and it would not take much more bad news to erase it. If appreciation is negative — if the home loses 2% of its value a year, for example — the value after 5 years is $361,568.32, the rent-path total falls to $105,954.05, and renting ends $20,045.95 behind selling now. A falling market flips the answer, and it flips it hard, because the largest force has changed sign.

Next, rent growth. In the example, rent grows at 3% a year, which is what turns the monthly cash flow from -$152.40 in year 1 into $110.87 in year 5. If rent does not grow at all and stays at $2,400 a month, the monthly cash flow stays at -$152.40 for all 60 months, and the total net rental cash flow over 5 years becomes -$9,144.00 instead of -$1,362.68. The rent-path total falls to $203,329.56 − $9,144.00 = $194,185.56, which is still $68,185.56 ahead of selling now — because appreciation and principal paydown are unchanged and they are doing most of the work. Flat rent hurts, but in this example it does not flip the answer. What would flip it is a combination: flat rent and no appreciation together leave only the principal paydown of $17,442.51 against a cash-flow drag of $9,144.00, a gap of $8,298.51 — still ahead, but only just. The lesson is that no single assumption has to be wrong for the margin to shrink; several small disappointments together can do it.

Then the horizon. A shorter horizon gives appreciation and principal paydown less time to build up, and it gives the early negative cash flow years more weight. Over 1 year instead of 5, on the same example values, the home is worth $412,000, the mortgage balance is $246,916.11, selling after 1 year leaves $140,363.89, and the rent-path total is $140,363.89 − $1,828.80 = $138,535.09 — still $12,535.09 ahead of selling now, but a much smaller margin than the 5-year figure of $75,966.88. The decomposition shows why: appreciation after selling costs is only $11,280.00 after 1 year, principal paid is only $3,083.89, and the cash flow is -$1,828.80, which is the whole of year 1's losses with no later positive years to offset them. A longer horizon is not automatically better — it also means more years of landlord risk, which the next section covers — but in pure arithmetic, time is what lets the two positive forces outgrow the early cash-flow losses.

Finally, selling costs. If selling costs are 10% instead of the example's 6%, selling now leaves $400,000 × 90% − $250,000 = $110,000.00, and selling after 5 years leaves $184,781.18, so the rent-path total is $183,418.50 — still $73,418.50 ahead of selling now. Higher selling costs hurt both sides, and they hurt the later sale slightly more in dollars, because the later sale is on a larger value, but in this example they do not come close to flipping the answer. That said, selling costs deserve a careful look in your own case, because they are the one assumption you can partly control: preparation work, concessions, and the terms you agree can all move the figure, and a selling cost far above the example's 6% would narrow the gap further. The honest approach is to use a realistic selling-cost figure for your own home, on both sides of the comparison, and to see the result before deciding.

The pattern across all four what-ifs is the same, and it is worth stating plainly. In this worked example, the answer is robust: it survives 0% appreciation, flat rent, a 1-year horizon, and 10% selling costs, each on its own. It does not survive a falling home value, and it would not survive several disappointments at once. Your own numbers may be more or less robust than that, and the only way to know is to test them the same way — change one assumption at a time on the Rent or Sell Calculator and watch where the answer flips. If it flips easily, the decision is genuinely close, and the non-financial factors — your time, your tolerance for risk, and whether you need the money soon — should carry more weight. If it holds up under every test, the arithmetic is giving you a clear signal, and you can weigh the risks of being a landlord against a margin you actually trust.

The risks of being a landlord

The arithmetic in this guide counts dollars, but being a landlord also costs time, attention, and peace of mind, and none of that appears in the cash-flow table. This section names the risks plainly, because a decision that looks good on paper can still be the wrong decision for a particular owner.

Vacancy is the first risk, and it is larger in real life than the example's neat 5% suggests. The example assumes the home is empty 5% of the time — roughly three weeks a year — and that the empty weeks are spread evenly. In practice, vacancy comes in lumps: a tenant leaves, the home sits empty for a month or two while you clean, repair, advertise, and screen new applicants, and during those months the mortgage, taxes, and insurance still have to be paid with no rent coming in. A single two-month vacancy costs $4,800 of rent at the example's $2,400 a month, and it also costs the management fee, the advertising, and your time. The example's 5% is a labelled assumption, not a promise, and if your area has slow tenant demand or your home needs work between tenants, the real figure could be worse. Before deciding, ask yourself how many months without rent you could carry without strain — because the months without rent are when the mortgage still arrives.

Repairs are the second risk, and they are the hardest to budget for honestly. The example sets maintenance at $2,400 a year, spread evenly at $200 a month, but real repairs do not arrive evenly. A roof, a furnace, a water heater, or a sewer problem can each cost several thousand dollars in a single bill, and a home that is ten or twenty years old is more likely to produce one of those bills during your 5-year horizon than a new home is. There is also the damage a bad tenant can leave behind, which the security deposit may not fully cover — and the rules on deposits, including how much can be charged and how quickly it must be returned, vary by state, so check the Security Deposit Calculator and the rules for your state before you count on the deposit to protect you. A sensible landlord keeps a cash reserve for repairs, separate from the rent, and treats the maintenance figure in the calculator as an average to budget for, not a maximum that will never be exceeded.

Tenant duties are the third risk, and they are about your time and your legal responsibilities rather than money alone. As a landlord, you are responsible for keeping the home habitable, for responding to repair requests within the time your state requires, for following the law on screening, deposits, notices, and eviction, and for handling disputes — including disputes with a tenant who stops paying. Screening matters here: a careful screening process, including checking the applicant's rental history, reduces the risk of a bad tenancy, but no screening process removes it entirely. The Rent Rules Explained guide covers the state rules that apply to rent and tenancies, and the Rent Increase Legality Checker covers rent increases in the states it supports — but neither replaces reading the rules for your own state and city, or getting advice when a situation is serious. If the idea of handling a non-paying tenant, a legal notice, or an eviction process fills you with dread, that is useful information about the decision, and a property manager only removes part of it: you still own the home, and the hard decisions are still yours.

Being a long-distance landlord is a risk of its own, and it deserves its own paragraph, because it changes every other risk. If you are moving to another city or another state, you cannot drive past the home, meet a tenant in person, check a repair with your own eyes, or show the home yourself when it is empty. Everything goes through a property manager, a contractor, or a phone call, and each of those adds cost, delay, and a chance of misunderstanding. The example's 8% management fee assumes a manager is doing the work — but a manager who is slow to fill a vacancy, slow to chase late rent, or careless about repairs can cost you far more than the fee itself. Long-distance landlording can work, and many owners do it, but it works best with a manager you trust, a cash reserve for surprises, and a willingness to accept that you are running a small business from a distance. If you are moving far away and you do not have a manager lined up, selling now deserves a second look, whatever the calculator says.

None of these risks appears in the $75,966.88 gap, and that is exactly the point. The gap is a financial comparison on stated assumptions. The risks are the price of earning it. A $75,966.88 advantage over 5 years is worth very different amounts to an owner who lives nearby, has a cash reserve, and is comfortable managing tenants, and to an owner who is moving across the country, has no reserve, and dreads every phone call from a tenant. Read the arithmetic first, then read this section again, and decide with both in front of you.

When selling usually wins vs when renting out usually wins

No list of "usually wins" can replace your own numbers, but the patterns are consistent enough to be useful — as long as they are read as tendencies, not as rules. Selling now usually wins when the money is needed soon, when the home would rent for far less than it costs to carry, when the owner is moving far away without a trusted manager, when the home needs expensive work before it can be rented, or when the owner simply does not want the job of being a landlord. It also usually wins when the home's value is expected to fall, or when the mortgage is so large relative to the rent that the monthly shortfall would strain the owner's budget for years. In the worked example, none of those applies strongly — the shortfall is $152.40 a month in year 1 and turns positive by year 4 — which is part of why renting comes out ahead there. Change the mortgage payment to something much larger, and the same home would tell a different story.

Renting out usually wins when the rent comfortably covers the costs, when the mortgage is small relative to the home's value, when the owner lives nearby or has a manager they trust, when the owner has a cash reserve for vacancies and repairs, and when the owner is comfortable holding the home for several years. It also usually wins when the owner expects to return to the home one day, or when selling now would trigger a tax result that renting for a shorter period would avoid — though that last point is a tax question, and the tax notes in the next section explain why it needs a professional's answer, not a calculator's. The worked example sits in this second group: the rent nearly covers the costs from the start, the mortgage balance falls steadily, the home's value rises on the example's assumptions, and the 5-year margin of $75,966.88 is large enough to survive most of the what-if tests in the previous section.

There is also a middle group that deserves honesty: cases where the arithmetic is close and the decision should turn on the non-financial factors. If the gap between the two totals is small — a few thousand dollars over 5 years, rather than tens of thousands — then a single bad vacancy, a single large repair, or a single year of flat rent can erase it entirely. In that situation, the right question is not "which side is ahead by a little?" but "which side would I regret less if things go wrong?" An owner who sells and watches the home's value rise can regret selling. An owner who rents and spends 5 years dealing with tenants, repairs, and shortfalls can regret renting. Neither regret is irrational, and neither is captured by the calculator. When the numbers are close, choose the path whose worst case you can live with — and if you need help weighing the affordability side of the decision, the Rent Affordability Calculator and the Rent Affordability and the 30% Rule guide cover the income tests that apply when you are the one paying rent, while the 3x Rent Calculator covers the income multiple landlords commonly use.

Tax notes

Tax is the part of this decision where a calculator cannot help you and a guide can only point at the questions. This section mentions the tax notes that matter most, without computing any tax figure, because your tax result depends on your income, your filing status, your dates, and your records — none of which this guide knows. In every case below, talk to a tax professional about your own situation before you decide.

The first note is the Section 121 primary-residence exclusion. If you owned and lived in the home for 2 of the last 5 years, the exclusion can shelter up to $250,000 of gain for a single filer, or $500,000 for married filing jointly, and it can generally be used only once every 2 years. That exclusion can be the single largest tax factor in this decision, because the gain on a home sold after years of ownership can be large — but whether you still qualify depends on your dates, and renting the home out starts a clock. Talk to a tax professional about whether you qualify, and for how long, before you assume the exclusion will be there when you sell.

The second note is the roughly 3-year cliff that follows from the first. Because the exclusion requires living in the home for 2 of the last 5 years, renting beyond about 36 months after moving out can mean the home no longer meets that test, and the exclusion can be lost. That does not mean you must sell within 3 years — it means the tax cost of renting longer needs to be weighed against the financial gain of renting longer, and that weighing needs a professional who can see your dates and your gain. A decision to rent for 5 years that ignores this cliff could give up a tax shelter worth far more than the rental margin. Talk to a tax professional about your own timeline before you commit to a horizon.

The third note is depreciation recapture. When a home has been rented out, the depreciation claimed — or that could have been claimed — while it was a rental can be taxed when the home is sold, as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at a rate of up to 25%, and that part of the gain is not sheltered by the Section 121 exclusion. This is easy to miss, because depreciation is a paper deduction taken during the rental years, and its tax cost only arrives at the sale, years later. It does not necessarily make renting a bad idea, but it is a real cost of the rent path that the calculator's cash-flow and sale figures do not include. Talk to a tax professional about how depreciation and recapture apply to your own return.

The fourth note is the simplest: rental income is generally taxable as ordinary income. The rent you collect is income, and the expenses of renting out the home are a separate question from the income itself — what can be deducted, what records you need, and how the rental years affect your return are all questions for a professional, not for this guide. Keep careful records from the first month you rent the home out: the rent received, the expenses paid, the dates the home was rented and empty, and any improvements you make. Those records are what a tax professional will need, and they are much harder to reconstruct years later than to keep as you go. Talk to a tax professional about what you owe and what records to keep.

One more caution, because it is the most expensive mistake in this area: do not let a tax assumption decide the sale on its own. The exclusion, the cliff, and recapture are all real, and they all interact, and the right answer for one owner can be the wrong answer for another owner with the same home and different dates. This guide mentions them so you know to ask — it does not answer them, and it does not compute any tax figure for you. Talk to a tax professional.

Limitations

This guide is general information, not legal advice and not financial advice. It does not predict what any home will be worth, what any rent will be, or what any tax result will be. Every figure in the worked example comes from labelled example values — a $400,000 home, a $250,000 mortgage, $2,400 monthly rent, a 6% annual mortgage rate, 5% vacancy, 8% management, a 5-year horizon, 3% annual appreciation, 3% annual rent growth, and 6% selling costs — and your own values will be different. The what-if illustrations in this guide are arithmetic on those same example values, not forecasts. Selling costs, taxes, insurance, maintenance, vacancy, management, and rent growth all vary by home, by place, and by year, and this guide does not assert a figure for any of them beyond the example. The tax notes mention the rules; they do not compute any tax figure and they do not replace advice from a tax professional about your own dates, filing status, and records. Local rules on tenancies, deposits, notices, and rent also vary by city and state — check the official source for your city and state before acting. If your situation involves a large gain, a move to another state, a home with major repair needs, or any dispute with a tenant, consider getting advice from a qualified professional, because this guide cannot assess your individual circumstances.

Frequently asked questions

Should I sell my house now or rent it out?

It depends on your numbers, not on a general rule. In the worked example in this guide, a $400,000 home with a $250,000 mortgage, $2,400 monthly rent, and a 5-year horizon ends $75,966.88 ahead on the rent path, but that is one example with its own assumptions, not a recommendation. Run your own numbers on the rent or sell calculator, test the assumptions that could flip the answer, and check the tax notes with a tax professional before you decide.

How do I work out whether renting out my house makes money?

Work out the monthly cash flow first: take the rent actually collected after vacancy, then subtract the mortgage payment, taxes, insurance, maintenance, HOA fees, and management fees. In the worked example, year-1 rent of $2,400 a month becomes $2,280 after 5% vacancy, costs total $2,432.40, and the monthly cash flow is -$152.40, so the rent does not cover the costs in year 1. Cash flow alone is not the whole answer, because appreciation and principal paydown also count, which is why the guide compares the full 5-year totals.

What does it cost to sell a house?

Selling costs can include agent commission, closing costs, repairs or preparation before listing, and concessions to the buyer, and the total varies by sale. In the worked example in this guide, selling costs are set at 6% of the home value as an example figure, which is $24,000 on a $400,000 home, leaving $376,000 before the mortgage is repaid. Use the calculator to try your own selling-cost figure rather than relying on the example percentage.

Does renting out my house affect the capital gains exclusion?

It can. The Section 121 primary-residence exclusion can shelter up to $250,000 of gain for a single filer or $500,000 for married filing jointly, if you owned and lived in the home for 2 of the last 5 years, and it can generally be used only once every 2 years. Renting beyond about 36 months after moving out can mean the home no longer meets that test, so the exclusion can be lost. Talk to a tax professional about your own dates before you decide how long to rent it out.

What is depreciation recapture?

When a home has been rented out, the depreciation claimed (or that could have been claimed) while it was a rental can be taxed when the home is sold, as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at a rate of up to 25%, and that part of the gain is not sheltered by the Section 121 exclusion. Talk to a tax professional about how this applies to your own return, because this guide does not calculate any tax figure for you.

How long should I rent out my house before selling?

There is no single right length of time, because a longer horizon gives appreciation and principal paydown more time to build up, while also giving vacancy, repairs, and tenant problems more time to happen. In the worked example, renting for 1 year ends $12,535.09 ahead of selling now, and renting for 5 years ends $75,966.88 ahead, both on the same example assumptions. The tax notes matter here too: renting beyond about 36 months after moving out can lose the Section 121 exclusion, so talk to a tax professional about your own timeline.

Is rental income taxable?

Yes. Rental income is generally taxable as ordinary income, and the expenses of renting out the home are a separate question from the income itself. This guide does not calculate any tax figure, so talk to a tax professional about what you owe and what records to keep.
