The amount of a security deposit is one of the first large sums a tenant pays, and it is also one of the most state-specific. There is no single national limit. California generally caps the deposit as described below, Texas imposes no statutory cap on the amount, and for every other state the answer in this guide is Check official source. This guide explains the verified positions, what a deposit can lawfully be used for, and how to check the rule where you live.

California: generally one month, with a small-landlord exception

In California, AB 12 (Civil Code 1950.5) generally limits a security deposit to one month’s rent. There is a two-month exception for small landlords as defined in the statute, so a two-month deposit is not automatically unlawful in California — it depends on whether the landlord meets the statutory definition. Always check the current text of the statute and any local ordinance, because a city may impose a stricter limit. If a landlord asks for more than the limit, ask in writing which provision they rely on, and keep that reply.

Texas: no statutory cap on the amount

Texas imposes no statutory cap on the amount of a security deposit. That does not mean any amount is reasonable in practice, and it does not remove the other protections Texas law provides — notably the return deadline and the forwarding-address rules described in our Texas deposit guide. In a no-cap state, the lease amount, the local market, and any local ordinance matter even more, so read the lease figure carefully and compare it with what was advertised. Do not assume a cap from California or another state applies in Texas.

Other states: check the official source

For every state other than those with a verified rule on this site, the correct answer in this guide is “Check official source.” Deposit limits elsewhere may be one month, two months, or something else entirely, and they may depend on whether the home is furnished, how many units the landlord owns, or whether a local ordinance applies. Borrowing another state’s figure is the most common error in online deposit advice. If you cannot find an official source, a local housing department or tenant organisation is a better next step than a general web summary.

What can a deposit be used for?

Typically, a deposit may be applied to unpaid rent and to damage beyond normal wear and tear — for example, a broken window or a large stain — but not to ordinary wear such as faded paint or worn carpet in high-traffic areas. Cleaning charges, repair costs, and rent arrears should be itemised. A landlord cannot normally use a deposit to cover improvements or pre-existing damage. That is why a move-in checklist and dated photographs matter so much: they are the evidence that separates pre-existing condition from tenant-caused damage at move-out.

Protecting your deposit from day one

  • Photograph every room, including inside appliances and cupboards, on move-in day, and store the photos with dates.
  • Complete the move-in checklist in detail and keep a signed copy — do not leave it blank.
  • Ask for receipts for anything you pay for, and keep the lease and all payment records.
  • At move-out, clean to the standard in the lease, photograph again, and give a forwarding address in writing.
  • If part of the deposit is withheld, ask for the itemised statement and check each item against your move-in evidence.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a national deposit limit?

No. Limits are set state by state and sometimes city by city. California generally allows one month; Texas has no statutory cap.

Can a deposit be used for normal wear and tear?

Generally no. Deposits cover unpaid rent and damage beyond normal wear and tear, with the exact scope set by state law and the lease.

What is the small-landlord exception in California?

AB 12 provides a two-month exception for small landlords as defined in the statute. Check the statutory definition and official source before relying on it.
