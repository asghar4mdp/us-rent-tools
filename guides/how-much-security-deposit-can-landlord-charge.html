How Much Security Deposit Can a Landlord Charge?

Short answer

California: security deposit is generally 1 month's rent, or 2 months only for a small landlord as defined in the statute (AB 12, Civil Code 1950.5). Texas: no statutory cap, so the lease amount controls. Example: $2,000 rent in California generally caps the deposit at $2,000. Other states: check the official source.

Last verified: 2026-10-25. This is general information, not legal advice. City ordinances may be stricter and may override state rules. Check the official source for your city and state before acting.

The amount of a security deposit is one of the first large sums a tenant pays, and it is also one of the most state-specific. There is no single national limit. California generally caps the deposit as described below, Texas imposes no statutory cap on the amount, and for every other state the answer in this guide is Check official source. This guide explains the verified positions, what a deposit can lawfully be used for, and how to check the rule where you live.

California: generally one month, with a small-landlord exception

In California, AB 12 (Civil Code 1950.5) generally limits a security deposit to one month’s rent. There is a two-month exception for small landlords as defined in the statute, so a two-month deposit is not automatically unlawful in California — it depends on whether the landlord meets the statutory definition. Always check the current text of the statute and any local ordinance, because a city may impose a stricter limit. If a landlord asks for more than the limit, ask in writing which provision they rely on, and keep that reply.

Texas: no statutory cap on the amount

Texas imposes no statutory cap on the amount of a security deposit. That does not mean any amount is reasonable in practice, and it does not remove the other protections Texas law provides — notably the return deadline and the forwarding-address rules described in our Texas deposit guide. In a no-cap state, the lease amount, the local market, and any local ordinance matter even more, so read the lease figure carefully and compare it with what was advertised. Do not assume a cap from California or another state applies in Texas.

Other states: check the official source

For every state other than those with a verified rule on this site, the correct answer in this guide is “Check official source.” Deposit limits elsewhere may be one month, two months, or something else entirely, and they may depend on whether the home is furnished, how many units the landlord owns, or whether a local ordinance applies. Borrowing another state’s figure is the most common error in online deposit advice. If you cannot find an official source, a local housing department or tenant organization is a better next step than a general web summary.

What can a deposit be used for?

Typically, a deposit may be applied to unpaid rent and to damage beyond normal wear and tear — for example, a broken window or a large stain — but not to ordinary wear such as faded paint or worn carpet in high-traffic areas. Cleaning charges, repair costs, and rent arrears should be itemized. A landlord cannot normally use a deposit to cover improvements or pre-existing damage. That is why a move-in checklist and dated photographs matter so much: they are the evidence that separates pre-existing condition from tenant-caused damage at move-out.

Protecting your deposit from day one

  • Photograph every room, including inside appliances and cabinets, on move-in day, and store the photos with dates.
  • Complete the move-in checklist in detail and keep a signed copy — do not leave it blank.
  • Ask for receipts for anything you pay for, and keep the lease and all payment records.
  • At move-out, clean to the standard in the lease, photograph again, and give a forwarding address in writing.
  • If part of the deposit is withheld, ask for the itemized statement and check each item against your move-in evidence.

Worked examples: California at different rent levels

In California, the deposit limit is tied directly to monthly rent, so the dollar limit moves with the rent. The table below shows the general one-month limit at five rent levels, and the two-month figure that applies only where the small-landlord exception is met. Treat the two-month column as conditional, not as an alternative a landlord can choose freely.

Monthly rentGeneral California limit (1 month)Only if small-landlord exception applies (2 months)
$1,200$1,200$2,400
$1,800$1,800$3,600
$2,000$2,000$4,000
$2,400$2,400$4,800
$3,000$3,000$6,000

Worked check at $2,400 rent: a $2,400 deposit fits the general limit. A $4,800 deposit fits only if the landlord meets the statutory definition of a small landlord. A $5,000 deposit at that rent fits neither figure and should be queried, even if the landlord describes it as standard practice. At $1,200 rent, the same logic gives a $1,200 general limit and a $2,400 conditional figure; a $2,500 deposit would exceed both. The question to ask is never just “is this a round number” but “which figure in the table does this match, and what is the basis for that figure?”

Move-in totals: why the deposit figure matters in cash terms

A deposit is rarely paid on its own. Most tenants pay it alongside the first month's rent, and the combined cash needed on move-in day is what strains a budget. The table below is budgeting arithmetic, not a legal rule about what must be charged together; it simply adds the figures a tenant is asked to pay so the total is visible before signing.

The difference between the first two rows is $1,800 in cash on a single day, for the same home and rent — which is why the small-landlord question is worth asking before you pay. In Texas, the fourth row is lawful as far as the amount cap is concerned, because the lease amount controls, but still compare it with the advertised figure and check for a stricter city ordinance.

If you are asked for more than the California limit

Suppose rent is $2,000 and the landlord asks for a $3,000 deposit, describing the landlord as a small landlord. The general limit is $2,000 and the conditional limit is $4,000, so $3,000 falls between the two figures. That in-between position is exactly why a bare assertion is not enough: the deposit is lawful at $3,000 only if the exception genuinely applies, and it is over the limit by $1,000 if it does not. Ask in writing which provision the landlord relies on and how the landlord meets the statutory definition, and keep the reply with the lease. Do not accept a verbal assurance as a substitute, and do not pay the excess “to secure the home” without recording that you queried it, because an unrecorded payment is much harder to revisit later. If the landlord cannot or will not explain the basis, check the official source and consider local advice before signing.

A second example: rent $2,400, deposit requested $5,200. That exceeds even the two-month conditional figure of $4,800, by $400. In that case the exception cannot explain the figure, whatever the landlord's status, and the excess is visible from the table alone. A third example shows the opposite error: rent $3,000, deposit $3,000, tenant assumes it is unlawful because a friend paid less. At $3,000 rent the general limit is itself $3,000, so the deposit fits. Checking the table before objecting saves a dispute that was never there.

The small-landlord exception: questions to ask

The statute defines which landlords qualify, and this guide does not restate that definition from memory, since a paraphrase that drops a condition misleads. Instead, ask: does the landlord claim the exception, on what basis, is that basis documented, and does any city ordinance impose a stricter limit regardless? Keep the written answer, because a later dispute will turn on what was said and when.

Keeping deposit payments separate in your records

Keep a separate record for each payment: date, amount, method, purpose, and receipt or bank record. If a deposit is paid in installments, record each installment and the running total against the limit. If a payment is labeled differently — for example, as a fee — record the landlord's exact wording and check the official source and lease for how it is treated, rather than re-labeling it yourself.

What this guide does not tell you

Only California and Texas get a deposit limit here; the California small-landlord exception is left undefined, return deadlines stop at the two figures in the table above (California 21 calendar days, Texas 30 days with the forwarding-address trigger), and no ruling is offered on whether any particular payment counts toward a deposit limit. It does not cover pet-related or other separately labeled payments, furnished-home variations, or installment arrangements, because those questions turn on the lease and the official source for your state and city. It also does not decide what a deposit may be deducted for in any individual case beyond the general principle of unpaid rent and damage beyond normal wear and tear. For any of those questions, the answer here is Check official source, and the practical step is the same: ask in writing, keep the answer, and keep your payment records.

Sources

  • California AB 12, Civil Code 1950.5 — generally 1 month's rent; 2 months for a small landlord as defined in the statute; return generally within 21 calendar days.
  • Texas Property Code 92.103 and 92.107 — no statutory cap on the amount; return within 30 days, with the forwarding-address trigger.
  • For all other states, and for city ordinances and the treatment of separately labeled payments: Check official source — the state legislature's or city's official site and housing department pages.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a national deposit limit?

No. Limits are set state by state and sometimes city by city. California generally allows one month; Texas has no statutory cap.

Can a deposit be used for normal wear and tear?

Generally no. Deposits cover unpaid rent and damage beyond normal wear and tear, with the exact scope set by state law and the lease.

What is the small-landlord exception in California?

AB 12 provides a two-month exception for small landlords as defined in the statute. Check the statutory definition and official source before relying on it.

Does the California limit apply to the total of all deposits or to each payment?

Check the official source and ask the landlord for a breakdown of every amount charged. Do not assume that splitting a deposit into two payments changes the total that counts.

Can a landlord increase the deposit during a tenancy?

Check the official source and your lease. This guide states the limit at the time of charging and does not state a separate mid-tenancy rule.

Is a deposit refundable if I leave before the lease ends?

Check the official source and your lease. Early departure can raise separate questions about rent and deductions, so the deposit answer cannot be given without them.

What if my lease says the deposit is non-refundable?

Keep the lease wording exactly as written and check the official source before accepting that label. A label in a lease does not replace the statute, and a dispute will turn on the statute and the facts.

Should I pay a deposit before I have seen the lease?

As a practical matter, no: read the lease figure first, compare it with the amount requested, and get a receipt for anything you pay. Paying first and reading later removes your best chance to query the figure.

How does Texas having no cap affect my budgeting?

It means the lease figure is the figure to budget for, so compare it with the advertised amount and with your move-in total before signing. A higher deposit in a no-cap state is a cash-flow question as well as a legal one.

Same warning as above: this is general information, not legal advice — check the official source for your city and state.
