How to Split Prorated Rent With Roommates

Short answer: Daily rent is monthly rent ÷ days in the month. At $1,600 in a 30-day month, a roommate joining on the 16th pays only for days 16–30: $800 shared, or $400 each at 50/50, while the first roommate also pays $800 alone for days 1–15, giving $1,200 and $400.

The formula

  1. Daily rent = monthly rent ÷ days in the month.
  2. Days with fewer roommates: whoever was there pays the full daily rate.
  3. Days with everyone there: split the daily rate by your normal ratio (50/50, or by room size).

Worked example: roommate joins mid-month

Rent is $1,600 in a 30-day month. You moved in on the 1st; your roommate joins on the 16th.

PeriodCalculationWho pays
Days 1–15, you alone15 × ($1,600 ÷ 30) = $800You: $800
Days 16–30, both of you15 × ($1,600 ÷ 30) = $800, split evenlyYou: $400, Roommate: $400
Total$1,600You: $1,200, Roommate: $400

The common mistake is splitting the whole month 50/50 — $800 each. That charges your roommate $400 for two weeks they did not live there. Get the daily rate and partial-month total first with the Prorated Rent Calculator, then apply the day counts above.

Roommate moves out mid-month

Use the same logic in reverse. The departing roommate pays their share only up to their move-out date. The remaining roommate covers the rest of the month, unless the landlord accepts a prorated amount for the whole unit — confirm which applies in your lease, because the landlord's total is usually still the full monthly rent.

Three rules that prevent arguments

  • Agree on the method before anyone moves furniture in, and write it down.
  • Keep rent and utilities separate: rent can follow room size, but shared utilities are usually cleanest split evenly.
  • Recalculate when circumstances change — a room switch, a departure, or a new roommate — rather than letting an old split linger.

What splitting prorated rent means — and what it does not change

There are two different totals in a shared home, and confusing them causes most roommate arguments. The first is the landlord's total for the unit: the monthly rent in the lease, or the prorated amount the landlord charges for a partial month. The second is the internal split: how the people living there divide that total among themselves. A day-based split changes only the second total. It does not reduce what the landlord is owed for the unit, and it does not create a discount for days when nobody lived there. If the landlord charges the full $1,600 for a month, the roommates' shares must still add up to $1,600, however they divide it. If the landlord charges a prorated $1,200 for a partial month, the shares must add up to $1,200. Start every split by writing down the landlord's total first, then divide that total — never divide a figure you wish the landlord had charged.

Worked example: unequal shares, 60/40, in a 31-day month

Rent is $1,860 in a 31-day month, which gives a clean daily rate of $1,860 ÷ 31 = $60.00. Roommate A lives there all month. Roommate B, who has the smaller room, joins on the 11th and normally pays 40%. Days 1–10 belong to A alone: 10 × $60.00 = $600.00, paid by A. Days 11–31 are shared: 21 × $60.00 = $1,260.00, split 60/40, giving A $756.00 and B $504.00. Totals are therefore A $600.00 + $756.00 = $1,356.00 and B $504.00, adding to $1,860.00 exactly. The mistake to avoid here is applying 60/40 to the whole month, which would give A $1,116.00 and B $744.00 — charging B $240.00 for ten days before B moved in, and undercharging A by the same amount. The ratio applies only to shared days; solo days belong entirely to the person who was there.

Worked example: three roommates, one joins late

Rent is $1,800 in a 30-day month, daily rate $60.00. A and B live there from the 1st; C joins on the 11th. Days 1–10 are shared by A and B only: 10 × $60.00 = $600.00, split $300.00 each. Days 11–30 are shared by all three: 20 × $60.00 = $1,200.00, split $400.00 each. Totals are A $700.00, B $700.00 and C $400.00, adding to $1,800.00. Note how C's late arrival does not change A and B's relationship to each other for the first ten days: they split those days between themselves, and C is not retroactively charged for them. If a third roommate joins and the household also changes its normal ratio at the same time — for example, because rooms are reassigned — apply the old ratio to days before the change and the new ratio to days after it, and write both ratios and the change date down.

Worked example: a gap between one leaving and another arriving

Rent is $1,500 in a 30-day month, daily rate $50.00. B moves out after the 10th, nobody replaces B from the 11th to the 15th, and C arrives on the 16th. Days 1–10, A and B: 10 × $50.00 = $500.00, split $250.00 each. Days 11–15, A alone: 5 × $50.00 = $250.00, paid by A. Days 16–30, A and C: 15 × $50.00 = $750.00, split $375.00 each. Totals are A $250.00 + $250.00 + $375.00 = $875.00, B $250.00 and C $375.00, adding to $1,500.00. The gap days are the point of this example: they belong to whoever remained, unless the departing roommate agreed in writing to keep paying through the gap, or the landlord agreed to a lower total for those days. Do not silently assign gap days to the newcomer, who had not yet arrived, and do not silently assign them to the leaver, who had already gone, without an agreement that says so.

Worked example: unequal shares at $2,400 with a mid-month arrival

Rent is $2,400 in a 30-day month, daily rate $80.00. A has the larger room and normally pays 60%; B joins on the 13th and normally pays 40%. Days 1–12 belong to A alone: 12 × $80.00 = $960.00. Days 13–30 are shared: 18 × $80.00 = $1,440.00, split $864.00 to A and $576.00 to B. Totals are A $960.00 + $864.00 = $1,824.00 and B $576.00, adding to $2,400.00. This example is useful because a whole-month 60/40 split would give A $1,440.00 and B $960.00, overcharging B by $384.00 for twelve days of absence. When the rent is high, a mid-month arrival makes the day-based method worth hundreds of dollars, not a few dollars of tidiness — which is also why the calculation should be agreed before move-in day, when nobody is yet unpacking boxes and tempers are short.

When the landlord's method differs from the calendar

Roommates must split the landlord's actual charge, not a figure calculated on a different method. Suppose the landlord charges $1,860 for a 31-day month but uses a 30-day method on the statement. A literal 30-day split for a roommate joining on the 16th would be 15 days alone at $62.00 = $930.00, plus 16 shared days at $62.00 = $992.00, split $496.00 each — totaling $1,922.00, which is more than the landlord charged. That split is unusable, because the shares would exceed the bill. Instead, split the landlord's $1,860 by days actually lived there: 15 solo days are 15/31 of $1,860 = $900.00, and 16 shared days are 16/31 of $1,860 = $960.00, split $480.00 each, giving $1,380.00 and $480.00. The lesson is to anchor the split to the bill, and to use day proportions when the landlord's method would otherwise overshoot or undershoot the bill.

Room switches, utilities and deposits: keep them separate

A room switch mid-month is a ratio change, not a new tenancy. If A and B swap rooms on the 16th of a 31-day month at $1,240 rent (daily $40.00), and the larger room pays 60%, then days 1–15 follow the old room assignment and days 16–31 follow the new one; do not apply the new ratio to the whole month. Utilities should be a separate line altogether: rent can follow room size, while shared utilities are usually cleanest split evenly, or by the agreement the household actually made — and a mid-month arrival should not be charged for utilities used before arrival unless the household agreed that in advance. Deposits are separate again. Who paid what share of a deposit, and who receives what back when someone leaves, should be written down separately from rent, because a departing roommate's deposit share is not automatically the same as their last month's rent share. For deposit return deadlines, check the official source and the verified rules in Rent Rules Explained, rather than assuming the rent split decides the deposit.

Paying the landlord: one payment or several?

Agree, before the first partial month, whether one roommate collects and pays the landlord, or each roommate pays the landlord directly. A single collector makes the landlord's total easy to prove — one payment of $1,600, for example — but leaves the collector chasing late shares. Separate payments make each person's contribution visible to the landlord, but can leave a shortfall if one person pays late and nobody notices until the total is short. Either way, keep a simple monthly record: landlord total, each person's share, date paid, and method of payment. If a share is late, the household still owes the landlord the full total under the lease, so the remaining roommates need a written plan for covering a shortfall rather than discovering it on the due date. That plan is an internal agreement; it does not change the lease, and it should say so.

What a day-based split does not tell you

Day counts divide the rent; they do not assign legal responsibility to the landlord, decide whether a lease allows a new roommate at all, settle whether a departing roommate remains liable after leaving, or state what a state or city rule requires for deposits, notice or fees. Those questions depend on the lease and on the official source for your city and state, and a fair internal split cannot answer them. It also does not settle subjective fairness: a roommate who works nights, uses the kitchen more, or has a partner staying over may raise issues no day count can resolve, and those need a separate household conversation, not a more precise formula. Use the day count for rent, write the agreement down, and treat everything else as its own question.

Frequently asked questions

Should a roommate who moves in mid-month pay half the month?

No, not for the days before they moved in. Split by days as above.

What if roommates pay different shares normally?

Use the same ratio only for the shared days. Do not apply it to days when only one person lived there.

How do we split if the larger-room roommate joins late?

Combine both rules: solo days go entirely to whoever was there, and shared days are split by the room-size ratio. In the $1,860 example above, the late joiner with the smaller room pays 40% of the shared $1,260, which is $504, and nothing for the solo days.

Should a departing roommate keep paying until a replacement is found?

Only if the lease or a written household agreement says so. The day-based calculation alone does not extend a leaver's share past their move-out date, and gap days fall to whoever remained unless an agreement says otherwise. Check the lease and the official source before assuming either way.

How do we handle a gap between a leaver and a joiner?

Charge the gap days to whoever actually lived there during the gap — usually the remaining roommate — and write that down. At $1,500 in a 30-day month, five gap days cost $250, which is too large to leave to memory or goodwill.

Do we split the deposit the same way as prorated rent?

No, not automatically. Record deposit shares separately, including who paid what and who receives what back when someone leaves. Deposit return deadlines are a separate state-law question; check the official source and Rent Rules Explained.

What if a roommate moves in on the 1st but gets keys on the 3rd?

Agree which date starts the count — lease start date or key handover date — and use it consistently. A two-day difference at $60 per day is $120, so confirm the start date in writing rather than letting each person count from a different date.

What if one roommate pays the landlord late?

The household still owes the landlord the full total for the unit, so the other roommates need a written plan for covering a shortfall. The late payer's internal share does not shrink because it was late, and the landlord's total does not shrink because the household disagrees.
