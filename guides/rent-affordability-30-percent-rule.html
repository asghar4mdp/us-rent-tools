Rent Affordability and the 30% Rule

Short answer

The 30% rule says total housing cost should be no more than 30% of gross monthly income. Maximum rent = gross monthly income × 0.30. For example, $6,000 × 0.30 = $1,800. It is a budgeting guideline, not a law, and it ignores debts and local costs, so use it as a starting point, not a final decision.

This is general information, not legal advice. City ordinances may be stricter and may override state rules. Check the official source for your city and state before acting.

How much of your income should go to housing?

Housing guidance commonly uses 30% of gross household income as the line between affordable and cost-burdened: at or below 30%, housing is generally described as affordable; above it, a household is described as cost-burdened. The rule is a rule of thumb used in housing policy and budgeting guidance, not a legal cap on what a landlord can charge or what a tenant can pay. Your own budget — debts, transport, food, childcare, savings — may make a lower figure right for you, or a higher figure workable for a short period.

The formula, worked

Maximum rent at 30% = gross monthly income × 30%. At $6,000 gross per month, that is $1,800. At $4,000, it is $1,200. The rent-to-income ratio runs the other way: rent ÷ gross income × 100%. A $2,100 rent on $6,000 income is 35% — above the 30% line. Use the Rent Affordability Calculator to run both directions, including an optional net-income version, which is stricter because tax and deductions are already removed.

30% vs 2.5x vs 3x

TestFormula (income $6,000)Maximum rent
30% rule$6,000 × 30%$1,800
3x$6,000 ÷ 3$2,000
2.5x$6,000 ÷ 2.5$2,400

The 30% rule is the most conservative of the three, because it caps rent at just under a third of income, while 3x allows exactly a third and 2.5x allows 40%. A rent can therefore pass a landlord's 2.5x screen while still breaching the 30% rule — that does not make either figure wrong, but it means the renter should budget carefully rather than treat a landlord's approval as proof of affordability. Gross-income tests also flatter the picture: on a net income of, say, $4,800, the 30% maximum is only $1,440.

What the 30% rule leaves out

The rule counts housing cost only, and different sources count different things inside it — rent alone, or rent plus utilities, insurance, and parking. Be explicit about what your figure includes, or two budgets will not be comparable. It also ignores debt payments, variable income, and local costs: in a high-cost city, many households pay above 30% because no home at 30% is available, and in a low-cost area, 30% may still leave little after other essentials. Treat the rule as a starting point for a real budget, not as a verdict.

Practical steps

  • Run the gross 30%, 2.5x, and 3x figures, then re-run 30% on net income for the cash view.
  • List the rent plus utilities, parking, and any recurring fees — that is the true housing cost to test.
  • Subtract debts and essentials before judging whether the remaining rent figure is comfortable, not merely permitted.
  • If the only available homes breach 30%, compare total costs (including commute) with the Rent Compare Calculator rather than headline rent alone.

What counts as housing cost?

Before you apply the 30% test, decide what number you are testing. Some people test rent alone; others test rent plus everything that keeps the home running. The answer changes the result, so the choice must be made openly and then used the same way for every home you compare. If one home bundles utilities and parking into the rent and another charges them separately, comparing rent alone will make the bundled home look expensive when its total housing cost may be lower.

A practical housing-cost total starts with monthly rent, then adds the recurring items you cannot avoid in that home: electricity, gas, water, sewer, trash collection where it is billed separately, renter's insurance where you carry it, and parking where you need a space. Internet and streaming are household spending, but many budgets include internet in housing cost because a home without it is not workable for remote work; the key point is to be consistent. One-time costs such as application fees or moving costs belong in a move-in budget, not in the monthly 30% test, because they do not repeat every month.

Worked example: rent of $1,700 plus $120 for electricity and gas, $60 for water, and $50 for parking gives a housing total of $1,930. On $6,000 of gross monthly income, rent alone is 28.3% ($1,700 ÷ $6,000), which passes the 30% line, while the full housing total is 32.2% ($1,930 ÷ $6,000), which does not. That $230 of extras is the difference between a pass and a fail, and it is exactly why a headline rent can mislead. Run the test twice — once on rent alone and once on the full housing total — and treat the full-total result as the stricter, more honest figure.

Read the table in both directions. A household earning $4,500 a month has a 30% maximum of $1,350, so a $1,600 rent is $250 above the line and takes 35.6% of gross income. A household earning $9,000 a month can pay $2,700 at 30%, so that same $1,600 rent takes only 17.8%. The same rent is therefore affordable for one household and stretching for another, which is why affordability is always a ratio, never a single dollar figure that suits everyone. Annual figures work the same way: $72,000 of gross annual income is $6,000 a month, and 30% of the annual figure, $21,600 a year, is the same $1,800 a month.

Gross income versus take-home pay

The standard 30% rule uses gross income, the figure before tax and deductions, because that is the figure landlords and housing guidance most often quote. Your bank account, however, runs on take-home pay. If tax, retirement contributions, and other deductions remove a fifth of gross pay, a rent that looks like 30% of gross may be closer to 37% or 38% of the money that actually arrives. That gap is not an error in the rule; it is a reason to run a second, cash-based version.

Example: gross pay of $6,000 a month with take-home pay of $4,800 a month. The gross 30% maximum is $1,800, but 30% of take-home pay is $1,440 — a $360 difference. A $1,800 rent is 30% of gross and 37.5% of take-home pay ($1,800 ÷ $4,800). A renter who budgets only on the gross figure may find the rent harder to carry than expected, because tax and deductions do not pause when rent is due. If you have a choice, set your search ceiling at the lower of the two figures when cash is tight, and use the gross figure only as an outer limit. The Rent Affordability Calculator lets you run both versions side by side, so you can see the gap for your own numbers before you sign anything.

Combined household income

For a couple or a household with two earners, the income figure is normally combined gross household income, not one person's pay. Two earners at $3,500 a month each give $7,000 combined, and a 30% maximum of $2,100. That combined figure only works while both incomes continue; if one earner stops working, the same $2,100 rent suddenly takes 60% of the remaining $3,500. A household that depends on two incomes should ask, before signing, whether the rent would still be workable on one income for a few months, and how much savings would cover the gap.

What the 30% rule does not tell you

Debt is the rule’s blind spot, and debt is often what breaks a budget. A household with a $400 monthly loan payment and a household with no debt can both pass the 30% test at the same rent, but the first household has $400 less for everything else. Add debt payments, childcare, transport, food, and savings goals to the picture before calling a rent affordable. A useful check is the remainder test: income minus housing total minus debts minus essentials should still leave room for savings and for an unexpected bill. If the remainder is zero or negative, the rent is not affordable in practice, whatever the percentage says.

The rule also ignores place. A home at 32% of income with a short, cheap commute can cost less in total than a home at 28% with a long, expensive commute. Example: Home A rents for $1,680 (28% of $6,000) plus $250 a month in commuting costs, for a combined $1,930. Home B rents for $1,900 (31.7% of $6,000) with no commuting cost, for a combined $1,900. Home B breaches the 30% line on rent alone yet costs $30 less per month in total. Use the Rent Compare Calculator for that combined comparison, and do not let a single percentage override the total. Local prices matter too: in an area where no available home meets 30%, the rule becomes a warning to budget tightly and to weigh trade-offs, not a rule that makes housing appear out of nowhere. Check the official source for any local rule that may apply, and treat the 30% figure as guidance in that situation rather than as a barrier that must be obeyed at any cost.

Common mistakes

  • Testing rent alone while forgetting utilities, parking, and insurance, which can add several hundred dollars a month.
  • Using gross income for the test but take-home pay for every other bill, which understates how heavy the rent feels in cash terms.
  • Annualizing a single good month for variable income instead of averaging twelve months.
  • Treating a landlord's approval under a 2.5x or 3x screen as proof of affordability, when those screens allow higher rents than the 30% rule.
  • Comparing two homes on headline rent without adding commuting, parking, and utility differences.
  • Setting the ceiling at exactly 30% with no margin for a renewal increase or a utility rise.

Frequently asked questions

How much of my income should go to housing?

Housing guidance commonly uses 30% of gross income as the affordability line. It is a rule of thumb, not a legal limit.

Is the 30% rule gross or net income?

It is usually stated on gross income, but the net-income version is the stricter and often more realistic cash test.

What if I pay more than 30%?

You are described as cost-burdened in housing guidance. That is a budgeting warning, not a legal problem in itself — check your full budget before deciding.

Should utilities be included in the 30% calculation?

They should be included in your own stricter version of the test, because they are part of what the home costs each month. The standard statement of the rule is often made on rent alone, so state which version you used when you compare homes, and run the full-total version before deciding.

How do I calculate 30% of my income?

Multiply gross monthly income by 0.30. For $5,000 a month, that is $1,500. For annual income, divide by 12 first, then multiply by 0.30 — or multiply annual income by 0.30 and divide by 12; both routes give the same monthly maximum.

Is 30% based on one person's income or household income?

Household income: combine the gross incomes of the people who will pay the rent. Then test each person's share separately if roommates split the rent, so that no single person is carrying a share they cannot afford.

What if no home in my area meets the 30% rule?

Then treat the rule as a warning, not as a verdict that no move is possible. Compare total costs including commuting, look at smaller homes or shared housing, and build the tightest budget you can — and check the official source for any local rule or assistance that may apply in your city or state.

Does the 30% rule apply to a mortgage as well as rent?

The same 30% idea is used in housing guidance for owners, but the housing total there includes items such as property tax and insurance that renters do not pay in the same way. Do not compare a renter's rent-only percentage directly with an owner's all-in percentage without listing what each total includes.
