Late Fee Grace Period: When Can a Landlord Charge a Late Fee?

Short answer

A grace period is the days after the due date during which rent can still be paid without a late fee. Oregon (ORS 90.260) provides a 4-day grace period and requires the fee to be in a written agreement; other states vary, so check the official source.

If rent is a few days late, can your landlord charge a fee right away? The answer is: sometimes, but not always. A grace period is a number of days after the due date during which rent can still be paid without a late fee. Whether you have one, how long it is, and whether the fee must be written into your agreement all depend on state law and your lease. This guide explains the general principles and the verified Oregon rule, and shows you how to check your own position.

What is a grace period?

Suppose rent is due on the 1st and there is a 4-day grace period. Rent paid on the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, or 5th would not attract a late fee, because the grace period covers those days; rent paid on the 6th could. Without a grace period, a fee could in principle be charged on the 2nd, if the lease and law allow it. Grace periods exist to allow for weekends, bank processing, and ordinary delays, but they are not a right to pay late every month without consequence — repeated late payment can still lead to notices or, in serious cases, eviction proceedings under the relevant law, even if no fee was charged for a particular month.

Oregon: 4-day grace period under ORS 90.260

Oregon law, ORS 90.260, provides a 4-day grace period, and requires the late fee to be set out in a written rental agreement before it can be charged. The statute also uses a structure of 5% per succeeding 5-day period as one statutory structure for late fees. That wording matters: it should not be simplified into a single flat 5% overall cap, because the per-period structure can produce a different total depending on how late the rent is and how the agreement is written. If you rent in Oregon, read the fee clause in your written agreement, count the grace days from the due date, and then apply the per-period structure as written — or check the official source and the agreement together before paying a fee you are unsure about.

Other states: check the official source

For states without a verified rule on this site, do not assume Oregon’s 4 days apply, and do not assume there is no grace period either. Some states set a grace period by statute, some set a cap on the fee, some require the fee to be reasonable, and some leave the matter largely to the lease. A city ordinance may also apply. The safest approach is to read your lease’s late-fee clause, then check the official source for your state and city. If the lease and the law conflict, the law normally prevails, but that question can be technical — seek local advice if the fee is large or disputed.

How late fees are usually calculated

Common structures include a flat fee (for example, $50 per late payment), a percentage of monthly rent (for example, 5% of rent), and a per-period fee such as Oregon’s 5% per succeeding 5-day period. A flat fee and a percentage fee give very different results on high rents, and a per-period fee grows the longer rent remains unpaid. Whatever the structure, the fee should be stated in the agreement, the due date and payment date should be clear, and the landlord should be able to show the calculation. Keep proof of when you paid — a bank transfer receipt or a landlord’s receipt — because the payment date, not the date you intended to pay, usually decides whether a fee applies.

If rent will be late

  • Pay as soon as you can and keep proof of the payment date and amount.
  • Tell your landlord in writing before the due date if possible, and ask about a short payment plan.
  • Check whether a grace period applies and whether the fee is in your written agreement.
  • If a fee is charged during a grace period or without a written agreement term, raise it in writing with your calculation.
  • If late payment is becoming regular, seek budgeting or housing advice early — fees compound and arrears can escalate.

Formula: how to check a fee step by step

Use this sequence before paying or disputing any late fee. First, find the due date in the lease. Second, find whether a grace period applies and how many days it lasts — in Oregon, 4 days under ORS 90.260. Third, find the fee clause in the written agreement: is there one, what amount or percentage does it state, and does it charge once, per period, or in another way? Fourth, work out the payment date from your proof, not from memory. Fifth, count the days from the due date to the payment date, subtract the grace days, and apply the fee structure only to the days that remain. If any step cannot be answered from the lease and the official source, ask the landlord in writing to show the calculation.

Worked examples with dollar figures

Example A, inside grace: rent is $1,400, due 1 October, paid 4 October in Oregon. The payment falls inside the 4-day grace period, so no late fee applies under ORS 90.260. The tenant still owes the $1,400 rent itself, and should keep the payment proof showing 4 October. Example B, one period: rent is $1,600, due 1 October, paid 8 October in Oregon. The payment is outside grace, in the first succeeding 5-day period, so one period charge at 5% of periodic rent is $80, if the written agreement provides for that structure. The total due for that month is $1,600 rent plus $80 fee, which is $1,680, before any other amounts the lease may separately allow.

Example C, comparing structures: on $2,000 rent, a flat $50 fee gives $50 however late the payment is, a single 5% fee gives $100, and a per-period 5% structure gives $100 for one period, $200 for two periods, and $300 for three periods, as the periods accumulate. That comparison shows why the structure matters more than the headline percentage: two fees that both mention 5% can produce very different totals by the end of the month. Example D, partial payment: a tenant pays $1,000 of $1,800 rent on 3 October, inside grace, and the remaining $800 on 12 October. The fee question then turns on the unpaid $800 and the date it was paid, not on the $1,000 that arrived inside grace — check the agreement and the official source for how partial payments are treated, and keep both payment proofs separately.

Edge cases: weekends, partial payments, and repeated lateness

A grace period ending on a weekend raises a question this guide does not answer by assumption: does the tenant gain extra days, or does the fee start on the next calendar day regardless? Check the lease and the official source, and pay before the weekend where possible to avoid the argument entirely.

Payment timing needs care too: rent paid by bank transfer on a Friday evening may not reach the landlord’s account until Monday, and rent posted by check depends on delivery and clearing. The date that usually decides the fee question is the date payment was made or received as the lease and the official source define it, not the date you intended to pay — keep the bank confirmation or receipt with the date visible, and pay early enough for processing to land inside grace where the lease counts receipt.

What a grace period does not tell you

A grace period answers a narrow question only: whether a fee can be charged for payment in that window. It does not say rent is not due on the due date, it does not cancel arrears, and it does not set the fee amount — that comes from the agreement and, in Oregon, the per-period structure. It also does not transfer across states: Oregon's 4 days are verified here, but another state's rule may differ, and a city ordinance may add its own rule.

Frequently asked questions

What is a grace period?

A period after the due date during which rent can be paid without a late fee. Oregon provides 4 days under ORS 90.260; other states differ.

Must a late fee be in writing?

In Oregon, yes — the fee must be in a written rental agreement. Other states may have similar requirements; check the official source.

Is Oregon’s late fee capped at 5%?

Not as a single flat overall cap. ORS 90.260 uses 5% per succeeding 5-day period as one statutory structure, so the total depends on how late rent is and what the agreement says.

Does a grace period mean rent is not due until the grace period ends?

No. Rent is due on the due date in the lease; the grace period only affects whether a late fee applies for payment inside that window. Arrears still exist until the rent itself is paid.

What if I pay part of the rent inside grace and the rest later?

Keep both payment proofs and check the agreement and official source for how partial payments are treated. The fee question will turn on the unpaid balance and the date it was paid, not on the part that arrived inside grace.

What if my landlord charges a fee but there is no fee clause in my agreement?

In Oregon the fee must be in a written rental agreement. Raise the missing clause in writing, ask for the calculation to be shown, and check the official source before paying a fee you cannot trace to an agreement term.

Does paying on the last grace day by bank transfer count?

It depends on whether the lease and official source count payment when sent or when received. Keep the transfer confirmation and, where the lease counts receipt, start the transfer early enough for it to arrive inside grace.

Can a landlord charge a fee every month I pay a few days late?

A fee can only be charged where the agreement and law allow it for that month, after grace. A waiver in one month does not automatically waive later months.

What if my state has no grace period listed here?

Check the official source for your state and city and read your lease. Do not assume Oregon's 4 days apply, and do not assume there is no grace period either, because some states set one by statute and others leave the matter largely to the lease.
